# Human-in-the-Loop AI in Australian Disability Services: Opportunity Mapping for the NDIS Ecosystem

**Research Date:** 21 April 2026
**Depth:** Standard (5 parallel research streams + 1 gap-fill pass)
**Sources Consulted:** 38 distinct sources (Tier 1: 18 / Tier 2: 8 / Tier 3: 12)
**Frameworks Used:** Deep-research methodology + Double Diamond design thinking

---

## Executive Summary

The National Disability Insurance Scheme (NDIS) is in the middle of the largest reform cycle since its 2013 inception, with 739,414 active participants and A$46.3 billion in annual scheme payments [8]. Two structural changes coincide with the maturing of generative and agentic AI: the *Getting the NDIS Back on Track Act 2024* introduces a "new framework" needs assessment using the I-CAN v6 instrument from mid-2026 [14], and Support Coordinators and Local Area Coordinators are scheduled to be replaced by a new Navigator workforce that will be available even to non-NDIS Australians under the Foundational Supports / Thriving Kids reforms [2][3][22]. Meanwhile, existing provider tooling has concentrated AI in three narrow zones — rostering optimisation, ambient progress-note scribing, and line-item validation — leaving substantial white space in coordinator workflows, transport orchestration, and supported-employment integration [1][3][4][15].

The dominant lesson from Australia's adjacent welfare-tech history is that AI deployed *without* a meaningful human-in-the-loop (HITL) layer collapses politically, legally and ethically. The Robodebt Royal Commission described automated income-averaging without human review as "crude, cruel and unlawful" [23][24], the NDIA's 2021 Independent Assessments proposal was scrapped after a cross-disability coalition mobilised against algorithmic budget-setting [11][12], and Services Australia received 414 formal review requests in the first months of My Aged Care's algorithmic Independent Assessment Tool [25]. Conversely, AI scribes such as Heidi Health (~50% Australian GP penetration, 59 minutes of clinical capacity returned per clinician per day) [26][27] and Lyrebird Health (PDQI-9 quality scores of 37.1/40 versus 34.6 for manual notes) [28] succeeded by leaving the human as the unambiguous decision-maker.

The opportunity for an NDIS-focused HITL AI play is therefore not to *make* decisions about people but to *prepare, translate, attest and surface* them. The most defensible single bet is a **Navigator/Support-Coordinator co-pilot** that drafts plan-review preparation packs, structures provider-matching options for participant approval, and maintains an auditable trail back to source evidence — built explicitly around the human override and the Code of Conduct [4]. The riskiest assumption to validate first is whether coordinators will trust an AI brief enough to take it into a real participant meeting, and whether participants experience the brief as empowering rather than as another opaque algorithm.

## Background

The NDIS Review's December 2023 final report *Working Together to Deliver the NDIS* made 26 recommendations and 139 actions, proposing a three-tier system: mainstream services, new Foundational Supports, and a more focused individualised NDIS for people with significant and permanent disability [1][2]. Foundational Supports — including the children's Thriving Kids stream — are jointly funded with states at A$4 billion over five years, with first services from 1 October 2026 and full national rollout by 1 January 2028 [3]. The Grattan Institute estimates the steady-state cost of Foundational Supports at approximately A$6.4 billion per year, and Commonwealth–state cost-share negotiations remain unresolved as of late 2025 [1].

Concurrently, the *Getting the NDIS Back on Track Act 2024* introduced Section 32L "new framework plans" underpinned by a needs assessment tool. The NDIA selected the **Instrument for the Classification and Assessment of Support Needs (I-CAN v6)** in 2025 to operationalise that assessment from mid-2026 [14][15]. Disability advocates — including Every Australian Counts, People with Disability Australia (PWDA), the Australian Federation of Disability Organisations (AFDO), the First Peoples Disability Network (FPDN) and Inclusion Australia — have publicly demanded transparency on the algorithmic operations that will translate I-CAN scores into budgets, with the chair of Every Australian Counts stating that "the NDIS must never reduce us to data points in a secret algorithm" [16][29]. Disability Representative Organisations have specifically called for transparency on what they term "Computer-Generated NDIS Plans" [29].

Australia's AI governance environment has tightened in parallel. The **Policy for the Responsible Use of AI in Government** (DTA, v2.0 December 2025) is mandatory across Australian Public Service agencies and requires AI transparency statements and named accountable officers [9]. The **National Framework for the Assurance of AI in Government** (June 2024) sets five cornerstones: Governance, Data Governance, a Risk-Based Approach, Standards, and Procurement [8]. The **Voluntary AI Safety Standard** (September 2024) issued ten guardrails, with a companion proposal foreshadowing mandatory ex-ante obligations for "high-risk AI" — a category that almost certainly captures welfare and disability decisioning when implemented [10]. The NDIS Quality and Safeguards Commission has explicitly stated that the NDIS Code of Conduct, Practice Standards and the NDIS Act apply equally to AI use, that providers must tell participants when AI is being used, and that automation must not reduce informed choice or dignity of risk [4]. The Commission is itself building a A$160M Data and Regulatory Transformation (DART) "intelligent risk engine" — explicitly characterised as HITL ("not an automated enforcement system") with progressive rollout to a final handover in May 2027 [4][5][30].

Set against this regulatory tightening is a sector under acute operational stress. NDIS workforce turnover sits at 14–25% per year, around three times the broader Australian workforce, with approximately 45,900 leavers per year and an estimated 83,000 additional care workers needed [31]. The 15% aged-care pay rise drove a 42% increase in disability support workers exploring aged-care roles in the 90 days after the Fair Work decision [31]. NDIS Provider Outlook Report data cited by sector commentators indicates 76% of providers say navigating the system takes time away from care and 63% cite excessive regulatory requirements [32]. Appeals against NDIA decisions to the Administrative Appeals Tribunal (now the Administrative Review Tribunal) rose more than 700% from 2016 to 2023 [13], and a reported 73% of the 7,132 cases appealing NDIA decisions in the year to June 2025 succeeded in changing the decision [16].

This is the terrain on which any HITL AI proposition must be defensible.

## Key Findings

### Finding 1 — Australian policy is converging on "AI as drafting assistant, never as decision-maker"

The combined weight of recent Australian regulation and case law has narrowed where AI can sit in the disability-services value chain. The NDIS Quality and Safeguards Commission's AI Transparency Statement is explicit: providers must disclose AI use to participants, safeguard sensitive data, and ensure automation does not erode informed choice or dignity of risk; AI "is increasingly being used to develop behaviour support plans — but it cannot replace human judgement or oversight" [4]. A tribunal precedent involving an allied health practitioner who used AI to draft NDIS reports has already established that the human practitioner is "fully responsible and accountable" for the output, with AI permitted only as a drafting tool [6].

The *NDIS (Restrictive Practices and Behaviour Support) Rules 2018* require a registered NDIS behaviour support practitioner — necessarily a human, deemed by the Commission — to assess the participant and develop the Behaviour Support Plan; regulated restrictive practices require state or territory authorisation and monthly human reporting to the Commission [20][21]. Disability Royal Commission Recommendation 6.35 calls for nationally consistent legal frameworks for the authorisation, review and oversight of restrictive practices, again explicitly requiring human authorisation processes [18]. A national disability support worker registration scheme is recommended for commencement by 1 July 2028 [19].

The Robodebt Royal Commission's 2023 report describes income-averaging conducted without human review as "crude, cruel and unlawful," forming the canonical Australian cautionary tale for automated welfare decisioning [23][24]. The 2021 Independent Assessments proposal — which would have used a standardised tool feeding an algorithm that generated more than 400 "personas" linked to presumptive budgets — was scrapped after a 50,000-signature petition, a 20-organisation coalition and state-minister refusal [11][12]. Freedom-of-Information material reportedly indicated that the 2019 Tune Review's draft was amended by public servants to support mandatory independent assessments [11], underlining how easily algorithmic governance can be retro-fitted to look like a recommendation. In My Aged Care, the Independent Assessment Tool that underpins the Support at Home program (which replaced Home Care Packages on 1 November 2025) has drawn 414 formal review requests; workers have called it "cruel" and "inhumane" because it strips clinical judgement [25][33].

The directional implication is unmistakable. Any product that allows an algorithm to *make* a binding decision about a participant — funding level, eligibility, restrictive practice, employment classification — sits on the wrong side of both Australian regulation and Australian political memory. Conversely, products that visibly keep a named human in the loop, leave an auditable evidence trail, and respect the Code of Conduct have a clear path. The succession of Heidi Health and Lyrebird Health in clinician scribing illustrates the pattern: ambient capture and structured drafting, with the clinician remaining the unambiguous author of every clinical record [26][27][28].

### Finding 2 — Existing AI tooling clusters in three narrow zones; coordinators, transport and employment are white space

A vendor scan of the dominant NDIS provider software platforms reveals that almost all current AI investment sits in three places. **Rostering and SCHADS award optimisation** is led by CareMaster's AI Roster Optimiser (launched May 2025, claiming a ~16% payroll cost reduction) [4-vendor], Lumary DC (Salesforce-based with embedded SCHADS interpretation) [3-vendor], Vertex360's Premium "AI Scheduler" at A$17/user/month [1-vendor], and Yes AI integrating across Lumary, SupportAbility and ShiftCare with reported 38% reduction in rostering conflicts and ~A$52,000 average annual savings per provider [15-vendor]. **Ambient AI progress-note scribing** is led by Heidi Health (Australian-built, processing approximately one million consultations weekly globally and used by approximately 50% of Australian GPs) [26][27][34] and Lyrebird Health (10,000+ clinicians, 28,000+ daily consults, all Australian customer data stored in Australia) [28][35]; Splose offers a A$27/user/month + A$27 AI add-on aimed at allied health [6-vendor]. **Line-item / claim validation** is the third cluster, with Brevity offering strong PRODA and pricing-guide automation [2-vendor], and Lumary PM automating plan-manager claim validation [3-vendor].

Outside those three zones, the white space is striking:

- **Support Coordinators and the future Navigator role** are barely served by mainstream provider software. Most platforms target *direct service providers*, not the intermediary professionals whom the NDIS Review explicitly proposes to redesign — the recommended Navigator workforce (General, Specialist, Psychosocial Recovery, Housing & Living) is intended to replace Support Coordinators and Local Area Coordinators and to be available even to non-NDIS participants under Foundational Supports [2][22].
- **Transport-domain operations** are essentially unserved. Incumbent rostering platforms treat NDIS transport as an add-on line item rather than a domain. Transport funding is capped at A$1,784 / A$2,676 / A$3,456 per year for Levels 1/2/3 [5-participant] — easily exhausted in regional areas — and pain points concentrate around wheelchair-accessible vehicle shortages, no-shows, the reimbursement-after-payment cashflow burden, and the provider-travel versus activity-based-transport distinction [6-participant].
- **Disability Employment Services to NDIS integration** is unaddressed by major vendors. DES was replaced on 1 November 2025 by Inclusive Employment Australia (IEA), a tiered specialist/generalist model with A$227.6M extra funding, removal of the two-year service limit, eligibility extended to people with assessed work capacity ≤8 hours/week, wage subsidies up to A$10,000, and an entirely new IT platform that went live 3 November 2025 [33][7-employment]. Open employment outcomes for NDIS participants in the labour force sat at only 25% in Q1 2025–26 (up from 20% in 2021–22), with stark equity gaps — First Nations 12% versus non-Indigenous 26%; ACT 38% versus NT 19% [7].

A further white space sits inside the safeguarding architecture itself. The forthcoming NDIS Commission AI Risk Engine — explicitly HITL but assigning risk tiers to providers, workers, participants and networks [4][30] — will create a need for a HITL "appeal layer" through which providers and participants can contest, contextualise, or supply evidence to override algorithmic flags. There is no incumbent vendor positioned to build this.

### Finding 3 — Distribution and integration with the dominant workflow system are the decisive HITL adoption levers

The "AI scribe wars" in Australian general practice are the most informative recent precedent for HITL AI adoption in a regulated, fragmented, EMR-centric market. Best Practice — the dominant GP practice management system at approximately 80% market share — was the integration kingmaker. Lyrebird's deep native integration came via Best Practice's strategic backing in February 2024; Heidi countered by going freemium; Lyrebird and Best Practice then launched a free Lyrebird tier in response [36]. Heidi has reported 59 minutes of clinical capacity released per clinician per day and 97% of clinicians rating document quality good or excellent [27], closing a US$65M Series B in October 2025 at a US$465M valuation [26]. Lyrebird's PDQI-9 study reported notes scoring 37.1/40 versus 34.6/40 for clinicians' manual notes [28].

The pattern that produced this success has four ingredients: (a) the human stays the decision-maker, (b) the tool plugs into the dominant workflow system rather than asking clinicians to context-switch, (c) data sovereignty is explicit and Australian, and (d) freemium lowers adoption friction. Translating that pattern to the NDIS, the dominant workflow systems are PRODA (NDIA participant and provider portal), the NDIS Provider Portal myplace, and the major provider PMSes — Lumary, Brevity, Vertex360, ShiftCare, CareMaster, SupportAbility. A HITL AI play that can write back into a dominant PMS, surface evidence inside the existing coordinator daily workflow, and offer a freemium tier for sole-trader coordinators or small providers has a clearer path than a standalone application competing for a tab in the coordinator's browser. This is also the pattern that minimises Code-of-Conduct exposure — the human signs off inside a system already audited under the NDIS Practice Standards.

The contrasting cautionary pattern is Workforce Australia's digital services. The platform received A$295.9M over four years (2020–21) with A$860.4M in projected efficiency savings; the proportion of Workforce Australia Online participants in work or study three months after exit failed its benchmark for the rolling 12 months to 31 August 2025 [37], and the House Select Committee found compliance burden has crowded out tailored support, especially for the long-term unemployed who form the majority of the caseload [38]. Building the platform without solving the *human-mediated* relational layer underneath produced exactly the failure mode warned against by the Robodebt Royal Commission.

### Finding 4 — Participant trust in algorithms is fragile and unevenly distributed

Life satisfaction among NDIS participants aged 15–64 has improved from 37% in 2016–17 to 47% in 2023–24 — a 42% relative improvement — but the 53% who do not yet feel satisfied is the operative number when assessing appetite for new technology [39]. A 2025 qualitative study identified three overarching themes: "living a better life," "losing trust in the NDIS," and "hopes for the future," with participants describing "stressful and negative experiences due to inconsistencies and inefficiencies in plan reviews and allocation of funding" and the critical role of "allies" in navigation [40].

Crucially, the digital divide is itself uneven. Only 33.9% of people with disability have used generative AI compared with a 45.6% national average, and the Australian Digital Inclusion Index has flagged risks of "increased exposure to scams and misinformation" and "overreliance on AI for important decisions" [41]. The First Peoples Disability Network and Inclusion Australia, alongside PWDA and AFDO, have publicly demanded transparency on the I-CAN tool that will inform Support Needs Assessments from mid-2026 [29][42]. The 2025–26 Q1 employment-equity gap (First Nations participants in open employment at 12% versus non-Indigenous at 26% [7]) means any AI-mediated employment service that does not co-design with First Peoples Disability Network risks deepening rather than narrowing the gap. Adoption strategy must distinguish (a) participants who self-manage and are likely early adopters of AI-mediated worker-matching, from (b) participants who plan-manage and are likely to consume AI-mediated services indirectly through their coordinator, from (c) participants in cohorts (cognitive, communication, cultural) whose trust must be earned through co-design before any AI is in their workflow.

There is, however, an existing positive base on which to build. Be My Eyes (visual), Proloquo2Go (AAC), Live Transcribe (deaf/hard-of-hearing), AccessNow / WheelMate (accessible places) and Choiceworks (cognitive scaffolding) demonstrate that disability cohorts will adopt AI-augmented tools when the agency stays with the user [43]. The lesson is not that participants reject AI — it is that they reject AI that is done *to* them rather than *with* them.

### Finding 5 — The Navigator/Support-Coordinator transition creates a 2026–2028 product window

The NDIS Review's Navigator role is positioned to replace both Support Coordinators and Local Area Coordinators, with availability extended to non-NDIS participants under Foundational Supports [2][22]. National Disability Services has noted that the role's design is still in consultation, with the new Section 32L plans, the I-CAN v6 needs assessment, and Foundational Supports rollout converging on the same mid-2026 to 2028 window [14][15][22][44]. From a product standpoint, this creates an unusually large workflow disruption — incumbent coordinator software is built around the *current* Support Coordinator workflow, the new Navigator role will absorb additional system-navigation responsibilities for non-NDIS Australians, and the workforce being trained into Navigator roles will arrive without entrenched tool habits.

Three things follow. First, the addressable population for a Navigator co-pilot is substantially larger than the current support coordinator population because Navigators serve Foundational Supports cohorts as well as NDIS participants. Second, the Foundational Supports cost envelope of A$6.4 billion per year [1] suggests both Commonwealth and state governments will be looking for productivity tools to bend the curve, mirroring the rationale that drove Workforce Australia's A$295.9M build [37] — but with the Robodebt and Independent Assessments memory shaping procurement requirements toward demonstrable HITL safeguards. Third, the pricing-arrangements regime continues to tighten — the 2025–26 Pricing Arrangements (Support Catalogue v1.1, effective 24 November 2025) introduced 23 new intensive behaviour support items, removed 35 legacy items, increased DSW rates by approximately 3.95%, and removed plan-management setup fees [9-pricing], meaning that any AI co-pilot must be able to track a moving target line-item catalogue in real time.

## Cross-Cutting Analysis

Three patterns emerge when the findings are read together. First, the policy frontier and the operational frontier are converging on the same answer: HITL AI is the only politically and legally tenable design point in Australian disability services for the foreseeable future. The DTA's Policy for the Responsible Use of AI in Government [9], the NDIS Commission's AI Transparency Statement [4], the Restrictive Practices Rules [20][21], the Disability Royal Commission's restrictive-practices recommendation [18], the Robodebt Royal Commission's findings [23][24], the 2021 Independent Assessments collapse [11][12], the My Aged Care IAT review surge [25] and even the Commission's own DART risk engine framing [4][30] all push in the same direction. The risk for any vendor pursuing "AI replaces decision-maker" is not just regulatory — it is reputational, and the disability advocacy sector is exceptionally well organised at converting reputational pressure into political reversal.

Second, the white space is in *intermediation*, not *delivery*. The NDIS workforce shortage is real — 45,900 leavers per year, 83,000 additional workers needed [31] — but the binding constraint on participant outcomes appears to be the brittle layer of human intermediaries (Support Coordinators today, Navigators from 2026) who connect plans to providers to outcomes. AAT/ART overturn rates [13][16], coordinator burnout, the 76%/63% admin-burden statistics [32], and the participant qualitative findings about "allies" being decisive [40] all point the same way. The opportunity is to make those intermediaries faster, more consistent and more attestable — without making them less human.

Third, the distribution lesson from Heidi and Lyrebird [26][27][28][36] dovetails with the workflow-disruption window opening from 2026 [14][15][22]. A HITL co-pilot that integrates with the dominant PMSes (Lumary, Brevity, ShiftCare, Vertex360) and writes back into PRODA-touching workflows can ride two waves at once: the Navigator-role rollout and the I-CAN-driven plan-review workload spike. The same product positioned as "we replace your coordinator" would face existential adoption resistance; positioned as "we hand your coordinator their next 90 minutes back," it has Heidi-shaped tailwinds.

The interaction between the three patterns is the strategic insight. **Where Australian policy mandates a human, where the operational pain is acute, and where a workflow disruption is opening — the intersection is Navigator/Support-Coordinator workflow.** That intersection is also where the cautionary tales become tailwinds: a co-pilot that visibly shows the human is in charge differentiates against both the procurement memory of Robodebt and the participant memory of Independent Assessments.

## Limitations

Several limitations apply to this analysis. The Inclusive Employment Australia outcome data is not yet available because the program only commenced 1 November 2025 [33]; any concept that depends on IEA performance assumptions must be treated as forward-looking. Vendor-published productivity claims (CareMaster's 16% payroll reduction [4-vendor], Yes AI's 38% conflict reduction and ~A$52,000 average annual savings per provider [15-vendor], Heidi's 59 minutes per clinician per day [27]) are not independently audited; while the *direction* of the productivity gains is highly consistent across multiple vendors and adjacent sectors, the *magnitudes* should be discounted in business cases. The selection of I-CAN v6 is confirmed [14][15] but the public documentation does not yet specify whether NDIA planners will retain statutory override of I-CAN-generated budget bands, which materially affects the design of any participant-facing translator tool.

The 73% NDIA-decision-overturn figure for the year to June 2025 is sourced via advocacy commentary referencing Guardian reporting [16] and should be cross-checked against ART annual reports before being used in a pitch deck. The "~83,000 additional care workers needed" figure originates in a 2021 National Skills Commission projection [31] and is now older than 18 months on a fast-moving topic. The First Peoples Disability Network's specific position on AI in NDIS planning is captured indirectly through the Disability Representative Organisations joint statement [29]; deeper co-design engagement is essential before any First Nations–facing concept proceeds. Finally, the precise architecture of Inclusive Employment Australia's new IT platform is not publicly documented [33], which limits the ability to scope a specific integration concept for the employment domain.

Sources older than 18 months that nevertheless underpin specific claims are flagged inline: NDIS Review final report (December 2023) [1][2], Disability Royal Commission Final Report (September 2023) [17][19], Australia's AI Ethics Principles (2019) [7-policy], House Select Committee Workforce Australia report (November 2023) [38], 2019 Tune Review reference [11], and the National Skills Commission workforce projection [31].

## Design Thinking: Double Diamond Synthesis

### Framework Used

**Double Diamond.** The problem space is genuinely ambiguous (which audience, which domain, which job to anchor on?) and timeline is weeks not days. A GV Sprint would compress framing, and Jobs-to-be-Done switch interviews require recruiting recently-switched users that this exploration does not yet have access to. The Double Diamond's *Discover → Define → Develop → Deliver* cadence allows the research above to feed directly into divergence-then-convergence on both problem and solution.

### Discover → Define: Three Key Insights

> **Insight 1 — The trust deficit is the system constraint, not the workflow gap.**
>
> *Evidence:* Robodebt Royal Commission described automated decisioning as "crude, cruel and unlawful" [23][24]; the 2021 Independent Assessments proposal collapsed after a 50,000-signature petition [12]; My Aged Care's IAT drew 414 formal review requests in months [25]; AAT/ART appeals against NDIA decisions rose >700% since 2016 [13] with a reported 73% overturn rate in the year to June 2025 [16]; PWDA, AFDO, FPDN and Inclusion Australia have demanded transparency on the I-CAN-driven planning system [29].
>
> *Implication:* Any HITL AI product in this market must position the human decision-maker as the visible centre, with the AI as the visible assistant. Products that obscure the algorithm or remove the appeal layer will trigger the same political response that has already killed two prior automation efforts in this sector.

> **Insight 2 — The intermediary layer (Support Coordinators → Navigators) is the unloved bottleneck.**
>
> *Evidence:* 76% of providers say navigation takes time away from care; 63% cite excessive regulatory requirements [32]; participant qualitative research repeatedly identifies "allies" as decisive to NDIS experience [40]; current coordinator workflow is largely unserved by mainstream NDIS software platforms [3-vendor][1-vendor][2-vendor]; the NDIS Review proposes to replace coordinators with Navigators by mid-2026 to 2028 with expanded scope to non-NDIS Foundational Supports cohorts [2][22][44]; I-CAN v6 plan-review workload arrives in the same window [14][15].
>
> *Implication:* The product window 2026–2028 is unusually wide because the workforce is in transition, the workflow is in transition, and the tooling is in transition simultaneously. The intermediary is the right audience because they are the one who can be held accountable under the Code of Conduct and who already carries the Practice Standards register.

> **Insight 3 — Distribution beats sophistication.**
>
> *Evidence:* Heidi (~50% Australian GP penetration in 2025 [26][27]) and Lyrebird (10,000+ clinicians, all-AU data [28]) won the AI-scribe market not by best model quality but by Best Practice EMR integration, freemium pricing, and a clear data-sovereignty story [36]; Workforce Australia's A$295.9M build failed its benchmark despite generous funding because it did not solve the relational human layer [37][38].
>
> *Implication:* A standalone NDIS HITL AI app that asks coordinators to context-switch will lose to a worse product that ships inside Lumary, Brevity or Vertex360. Distribution strategy = integration strategy.

### Define: How Might We

> **HMW help an NDIS Support Coordinator (transitioning to Navigator) spend more of their hour with the participant and less on documentation, while keeping the participant in visible control of every decision the AI suggests?**

This HMW deliberately holds two non-negotiables in tension: a productivity gain that is large enough to drive adoption (Heidi's 59 minutes/day [27] is the benchmark to beat), and a trust posture that is visible enough to survive both the regulatory environment [4][9] and the participant-advocacy environment [16][29].

### Deliver: Recommended Concept

**Concept A — Navigator/Coordinator co-pilot for plan-review prep** is the recommended starting point. It sits at the intersection of where Australian policy mandates a human, where the operational pain is acute, where a workflow disruption is opening, and where the distribution playbook is already proven adjacent. It also creates a natural expansion path: once the coordinator workflow has trust, the same platform can layer Concept B (participant preference articulator surfaced via the coordinator), Concept E (I-CAN translator surfaced via the coordinator), and eventually Concept D (shift-evidence scribe captured under the same audit envelope).

### Conviction Level

**Medium-High.** The directional case is unusually well supported by the convergence of policy, operational pain, workflow disruption, and an analogous adoption playbook. What I cannot yet de-risk:

- *Would increase to High if:* (a) 3 of 5 cold-recruited Support Coordinators or Navigator pilots use a low-fidelity prep brief in a real participant meeting and report it changed at least one decision they would otherwise have made; (b) at least one major PMS (Lumary, Brevity or Vertex360) signals openness to a partner integration on commercially reasonable terms; (c) the NDIA confirms (in writing or via published guidance) that a Navigator may take an AI-generated brief into a plan review without breaching Code of Conduct.
- *Would decrease if:* (a) Heidi or Lyrebird ship an NDIS-coordinator vertical inside their existing distribution before the product window; (b) the NDIA publishes guidance restricting AI-mediated plan-review preparation; (c) PMS incumbents ship native AI co-pilot features bundled with their existing licences; (d) coordinator/Navigator payment structures under Foundational Supports do not leave room for tool spend.

### Prototype Plan

**Lowest-fidelity that tests the riskiest assumption** — a manually-augmented "Wizard of Oz" prep brief. The participant's coordinator uploads a redacted prior plan, the most recent six progress notes, and any provider correspondence. A human-plus-LLM workflow produces a 1-page brief structured as: *participant goals (verbatim) / current plan utilisation summary / flagged budget concerns linked to NDIS Pricing Arrangements 2025–26 line items [9-pricing] / suggested questions for the meeting / explicit "here is what the AI did not see" disclosure*. The coordinator reviews and edits before the meeting. After the meeting, the coordinator records (a) which sections of the brief they used unchanged, (b) which they edited, and (c) whether the brief changed at least one decision.

**Who to test with:** five Support Coordinators or transition-Navigators recruited via a single mid-size NDIS provider and one sole-trader coordinator network, deliberately oversampling for one participant cohort that Tier-1 advocacy organisations have flagged as high-risk for algorithmic harm (e.g. participants with complex communication needs, psychosocial disability, or First Nations participants — the latter only with explicit FPDN partnership). **Do not test directly on participants in the prototype phase**; only run participant-facing testing once at least three coordinators have completed two full cycles and consent to participant-facing introduction.

**Success criteria:** at least three coordinators complete a real plan-review cycle using the brief; coordinator-reported time saved is at least 30 minutes per review (half the Heidi benchmark [27], adjusted for higher edit rate in plan reviews vs. consults); at least one reported instance of the brief surfacing an item the coordinator would otherwise have missed; zero incidents reported under the participant-attestation step; and qualitative coordinator commentary demonstrating they felt *more*, not less, in control of the meeting.

---

## What to Build First

**Audience:** Support Coordinators transitioning to Navigators inside small-to-mid NDIS provider organisations and sole-trader coordinator networks. Australian only. Initial geographic focus on a single state to keep state-territory restrictive-practice variation manageable [20][21].

**Job-to-be-done:** *"Help me arrive at a participant's plan review meeting fully briefed in 15 minutes instead of 90, so I can spend the meeting listening to the participant rather than reading their file."*

**Riskiest assumption to validate first:** Coordinators will trust an AI-generated brief enough to take it into a real participant plan-review meeting, *and* participants — once shown the brief — will experience it as empowering (giving them visibility into what the system knows about them) rather than as a new opaque algorithm. Both halves of that assumption must hold; either failing kills the concept.

Everything else in the design space — the participant preference articulator, the transport orchestrator, the I-CAN translator, the shift-evidence scribe — should be deferred until that first assumption is either validated or invalidated.
